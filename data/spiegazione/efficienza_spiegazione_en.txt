TITLE|How YA-MS calculates the efficiency of a shower
SUB|Technical document - by Costantino IC8TEM - November 2026
H1|1. Introduction
H2|1.1 Purpose of this document
P|This document explains, step by step, how the program derives the efficiency of a meteor shower for a meteor scatter path. Each step states where it comes from: a published source, a check made on the program, or a choice made by the program itself. The complete, numbered equations are in the Manual of calculations (doc folder).
H2|1.2 How to read the labels
TAG|FONTE|the step follows a cited source; the number in square brackets refers to the list of sources at the end.
TAG|RILIEVO|comparison between the program and the sources, with the result of the check.
TAG|SCELTA|choice made by the program, not taken from a source: to be verified by on-air experience.
H2|1.3 What is meant by efficiency
TAG|FONTE|The efficiency indicates as a percentage how much the position of the shower allows reflections on a given path. The concept is due to Ilkka OH5IY (MS-Soft) [2] and is taken up in the article [1]: the efficiency maximum does not coincide with the visual maximum (ZHR), because it depends on where the radiant lies relative to the two stations.
TAG|FONTE|DL5MCG's MCG_MS [3] applies the same concept to four fixed directions (N/S, NE/SW, E/W, SE/NW) and, when the DX station's locator is entered, also to the real path.
TAG|SCELTA|YA-MS calculates the efficiency for the real path between the two locators, not for fixed directions.
H1|2. Where reflections take place: the E layer
H2|2.1 The shower trails
TAG|FONTE|The meteors of the shower produce ionised trails in the E layer, between 80 and 120 km in height. The SKiYMET radars give a mean height of about 90 km [1].
H2|2.2 The calculation height
TAG|SCELTA|The program reads the height histogram of the Collm radar [7] and uses the most frequent height (the mode) as the calculation height. If the "from radar" checkbox is unticked, the value entered by hand is used.
TAG|RILIEVO|Collm plot of 5/11/2026: mode 89.5 km, mean 88.6 km, standard deviation 6.8 km. The figure confirms the 90 km of the article [1].
TAG|RILIEVO|DJ5HG [18] and QTFGUI [17] use a fixed height of 102 km; the program prefers the value measured by the radar. Recommendation ITU-R P.843 [24] gives a mean height h = 124 − 17·log10(f) km: about 97 km at 36.2 MHz and 87 km at 144 MHz, in line with the Collm mode (89.5 km on 5/11/2026).
H1|3. The path geometry
H2|3.1 Earth, stations and midpoint
TAG|FONTE|Spherical Earth of radius r = 6372.7955 km [1].
TAG|SCELTA|The path is always the real one between the station's locator and that of the DX station. Distance, azimuth and midpoint are calculated with the great-circle formulas.
TAG|FONTE|Time-dependent calculations refer to the midpoint of the path: the best time can differ by up to 1.5 hours between the two ends (SM6FHZ [4]).
H2|3.2 Antenna elevation
TAG|FONTE|The elevation towards the reflection point is calculated with the formula of the article (Image 7) [1], with x = distance / r, H trail height, h station height:
EQ|e = 90° − 57,3 · arctan[ (r·tan x + (H − r(1−cos x)/cos x)·sin x) / (H − r(1−cos x)/cos x − h·sin x) ]
TAG|RILIEVO|The formula agrees within 0.03° with the exact spherical geometry. The program uses the two-argument arctangent, which gives negative values beyond the horizon and avoids the periodicity problem pointed out in the article.
H2|3.3 The common zone and its limits
TAG|FONTE|The article [1] identifies the intersections of the curves where the horizon of each station meets the E layer. They are the intersections of two circles around the stations, with radius equal to the range at trail height:
EQ|d_H = r · [ π/2 − e − arcsin( (r+h)·cos e / (r+H) ) ]
P|With H = 89.5 km and the lobe at 0° the range is 1062 km. If the path is longer than twice the range, the two stations do not see a common zone of the E layer.
TAG|RILIEVO|These two points are the farthest edges of the sky seen by both stations, not the points where most reflections arrive. On the path JN70CN - IN78UO (1710 km) they lie 37° to the left and right of the direct direction, whereas MSHV recommends 10°. This is why the program calls them "Limit A" and "Limit B" and draws them faintly on the map, with the two circles of the common zone.
TAG|RILIEVO|In the example of the article (JN44BB - IO53RT) the drawing places these intersections about 870 km from the stations: this corresponds to a lobe elevated by about 2° (865 km).
H2|3.4 Hot A and Hot B: where to point
TAG|FONTE|Reflections from sporadic meteors concentrate in two zones ("hot spots") on either side of the path midpoint. J. Richardson (AMS) [19], in the table quoted by DJ5HG [18], gives the antenna offset from the direct direction: 13° at 1000 km, 11° at 1250 km, 10° at 1500 and at 2000 km. AMS Bulletin No. 203, quoted by W8WN [14], gives 10° at 1500 and 1800 km.
TAG|SCELTA|Hot A and Hot B are the points at distance s from the midpoint, perpendicular to the path (A on the left looking from the station towards the DX station, B on the right). With the "auto (AMS)" checkbox ticked (the default) s comes from the offset in Richardson's table, linearly interpolated (13° below 1000 km, 10° above 2000 km): s = (D/2)·tan(offset). Unticking the checkbox lets you enter s by hand (MSHV [15] uses 150 km).
EQ|Δaz ≈ arctan( s / (D/2) )
TAG|RILIEVO|With the AMS table: 1000 km → 13°, s ≈ 115 km; 1250 km → 11°, s ≈ 121 km; 1500 km → 10°, s ≈ 132 km; 2000 km → 10°, s ≈ 176 km. JN70CN-IN78UO (1710 km) → 10°, s ≈ 151 km, like the 150 km used previously and by MSHV; on short paths the table brings the hot spots closer to the midpoint.
TAG|SCELTA|For the time shown on the Sky view the program sums, over all showers above the horizon, ZHR x efficiency split between side A and side B (chapter 7.1). The hot spot on the side receiving more reflections is the recommended one: it appears in red on the map.
TAG|FONTE|According to DJ5HG, quoted by DF5HC [16], reflection takes place on a cone around the trail: for shorter paths the antennas should be pointed to the side of the direct direction, both stations towards the same point, and depending on the radiant position one of the two points (HOT-A or HOT-B) is more favourable. DF5HC also gives the best distance as 1300-1500 km, with a maximum slightly beyond 2000 km.
H2|3.5 The antenna lobe
TAG|FONTE|Gain of a long Yagi from the boom length (DL6WU curve [10]) and −3 dB beamwidth from the gain (Kraus's rule [11]):
EQ|G = 7,773 · log10(L/λ) + 11,43 dBi          θK = √( 41253 / 10^(G/10) )
TAG|RILIEVO|W4RNL's NEC simulations [12] confirm the gain curve within 0.1 dB both for DL6WU Yagis and for those of David Tanner VK3AUU, which have a shorter boom for the same number of elements: the gain depends on the boom length. The same simulations give a horizontal lobe 5-10 % narrower than Kraus.
TAG|SCELTA|Horizontal lobe (the one for azimuth pointing) = 0.93 · θK; vertical lobe = θK. 14 λ Yagi at 432 MHz: 20.3 dBi, 18.1° horizontal and 19.5° vertical; W4RNL's models give 20.4 dBi and 18.4°/18.8°.
TAG|SCELTA|The antenna is always considered pointed towards the DX station. A hot spot that falls within ±θ/2 of that direction is already covered by the lobe: it is marked in grey instead of pink and the program shows a warning. The hot spot recommended by the efficiency nevertheless stays in red.
H1|4. The shower radiant
H2|4.1 Radiant position
TAG|FONTE|Right ascension, declination, solar longitude of the maximum, velocity, r index and ZHR come from the IMO 2026 and 2027 calendars [5], tables 5 and 7; the Antihelion source from table 6. The minor showers of the IMO list are also included, for example the October Camelopardalids (OCT, ZHR 5, active on 5-6 October), the Andromedids and the daytime radio showers of April and May: even with small ZHRs they contribute to the path index and to the hot spot recommendation.
TAG|SCELTA|For the daytime radio showers the IMO gives neither ZHR nor velocity: the program uses a working value of 5.
TAG|FONTE|Solar longitude (equinox J2000), sidereal time and conversion to azimuth and elevation: formulas of Meeus [6].
TAG|SCELTA|The daily radiant drift uses working values, written in the file sciami.csv and editable.
TAG|RILIEVO|Comparison with the catalogue of the IAU Meteor Data Center [32]: the Showers tab shows for each shower how many solutions are published in the list of established showers and the difference between the program's IMO catalogue and the median of the solutions: angular distance of the radiants (compared at the same solar longitude, with the program's drift), Δλ (the program's λ max minus the median of the mean solar longitudes of activity, which do not exactly coincide with the maximum) and Δv. The tooltip lists the solutions with their references. The original file is downloaded at most once a month from www.ta3.sk; the MDC asks to cite Jenniskens et al. 2020 and Jopek and Kaňuchová 2017.
H2|4.2 Date of the maximum
TAG|FONTE|The program always shows the next maximum. When the IMO has published the calendar for that year (2026 and 2027 are already included in massimi_imo.csv) it uses the IMO date and time [5]; otherwise it calculates the instant at which the Sun reaches the longitude of the maximum.
TAG|RILIEVO|The calculation agrees with the IMO 2026 and 2027 calendars within a few minutes for the showers for which the IMO gives the time (for example Quadrantids 3/1/2026 21:13 versus 21:00, Geminids 14/12/2027 19:51 versus 20:00). The exceptions are the daytime kappa-Leonids 2026, one day later (26/9 instead of 25/9), and the time of the eta-Aquariids 2027 (15:10 versus the IMO's 09:00): in these cases the IMO value applies.
H2|4.3 Activity over time
TAG|FONTE|Maximum ZHR from the IMO calendar [5].
TAG|SCELTA|Around the maximum the activity decreases exponentially, with different slopes on the rise and on the decline (working values in sciami.csv):
EQ|ZHR(λ) = ZHRmax · 10^( −B · |λ − λmax| )
H1|5. The reflection condition
H2|5.1 Specular reflection
TAG|FONTE|A trail sends the signal between the two stations only if it is tangent to an ellipsoid with the stations at its foci (McKinley [8]). The trails of a shower are parallel to the radiant direction [1, 8].
P|In practice, for each point P of the E layer the directions from P towards the two stations and their bisector n are taken. The trail, parallel to the radiant (direction s), reflects towards both stations when it is perpendicular to the bisector:
EQ|s · n = 0
TAG|FONTE|The same law underlies DJ5HG's QTFGUI: the ray that hits the trail is scattered on a cone around it, and only those on the cone receive the signal. The cone condition is equivalent to s · n = 0. [17, 18] In his 2004 text DJ5HG describes it like this: the trail crosses the E layer, the two antennas illuminate it and the receiver lies on the cone that has the trail as its axis; the wall of the cone is the lobe of a radiator thousands of wavelengths long, hence narrow, but at grazing incidence (long paths) it touches the ground over a wide area.
H2|5.2 Tolerance
TAG|SCELTA|The condition is accepted with a Gaussian tolerance of 0.05 rad (about 3°). For an ideal trail the wall of the cone is much narrower [18]; the 3° represent what the ideal cone does not see: the spread of the radiants of a shower (0.3-1.4° measured by the GMN), the trail bent by the wind and its finite length.
TAG|RILIEVO|For meteor scatter DJ5HG's QTFGUI draws deviations from the reflection law of up to 32°: the program's Gaussian tolerance of 0.05 rad is stricter and concentrates the weight on almost exactly specular points. With 5 km sampling instead of 15 km the percentages change by less than 0.3 points.
H2|5.3 Practical consequence: the best pointing
TAG|FONTE|A trail sends the signal between the two stations only if it is tangent to an ellipsoid with the stations at its foci: at each point of the E layer only the radiants on the semicircle of the sky perpendicular to the bisector of the scattering angle are useful (Eshleman and Mlodnosky [20]; Meeks and James [21]). Near the midpoint this semicircle rises highest across the path: a radiant at elevation El produces specular trails about H·tan El to the side of the midpoint, on the side opposite the radiant, i.e. in the hot spots that Eshleman and Mlodnosky and Recommendation ITU-R P.843 [24] place about 100 km on either side. The number of trails and their ionisation increase with sin El [21]. The dependence of the echoes on the radiant position was calculated by Hines [22] and verified on four showers by Forsyth, Hines and Vogan [23]. From this follows the practical rule of SM6FHZ [4]: best path orthogonal to the radiant azimuth, pointing Az ± 90°, tolerance ±40-50°.
TAG|RILIEVO|Reservation by DJ5HG [18]: with a low radiant lying right along the path McKinley's formula gives strong signals, even though the meteors are few (Hamburg-Narvik, Quadrantids, around 19 UTC). The rule "radiant aligned with the path = almost zero efficiency" therefore holds for a high radiant, not for a grazing one. The program stays cautious and does not raise the efficiency, but shows "LOW RADIANT ALONG THE PATH" on the Sky view when an active shower has its radiant below 20° and within 30° of the path direction. The gap with the radiant at the zenith between the two stations is confirmed (Quadrantids, Hamburg-Barcelona, around 08 UTC).
TAG|RILIEVO|The same rule appears in the formula of DH9GCD quoted by DF5HC [16]: effectiveness = sin(El) · cos(El) · sin(P − Az), with El and Az the elevation and azimuth of the radiant and P the azimuth of the DX station. It is maximum with the radiant at 45° and across the path; DF5HC considers the radiant too low below 20° and too high above 70°. The formula takes no account of distance or heights, but it is a good term of comparison: in the sub-tab "Forecast calculation › Efficiency calculation" it appears in light blue under each shower, as a percentage of its maximum (0.5). When the radiant is above 70° as seen from the station the program flags it with ↑ in the table and with a warning on the Sky view.
TAG|RILIEVO|The DH9GCD formula has no published primary source: it combines the factor sin El (number and ionisation of the trails) with a factor cos El that represents how far the specular point moves away from the centre of the path as the radiant rises. Calculated with the radiant seen from the midpoint, as MS-Soft [25] does, it reproduces the MS-Soft table for KP30HV-JO52MM (1324 km, Quadrantids, 4/1/2001) within 4 points between 16° and 48° of elevation and within 16 points up to 64°, with the same maximum at 13 UT (96 % versus 92 %). It remains a term of comparison.
H1|6. From the single point to the efficiency
H2|6.1 The reflection points
TAG|SCELTA|The program divides the E layer into points every 15 km and keeps only those that both stations see above the minimum elevation of the lobe.
H2|6.2 The weight of each point
TAG|FONTE|The power of an underdense trail (McKinley [8]; ITU-R P.843 [24]) increases with λ³ and decreases with the distances R1, R2 from the stations; φ is half the scattering angle, β the angle between the trail and the plane containing the two stations and the point:
EQ|weight = 1 / [ R1 · R2 · (R1 + R2) · (1 − cos²β · sin²φ) ]
TAG|SCELTA|Up to version 1.6 the program took β = 0 for all trails (weight with cos²φ): trails crossing the plane of the path weighed up to 10-15 dB too much. From 1.7 β can be calculated for each point and each radiant direction with the option "Angle β between trail and path (full McKinley)", on by default because it gives the most cautious values (no trail is overestimated).
TAG|SCELTA|The angle φ is limited to 85° to avoid infinite values in grazing directions.
TAG|FONTE|The number of meteors arriving at a point is proportional to the sine of the radiant elevation at that point: it is the same correction with which the IMO calculates the ZHR [5].
TAG|SCELTA|Option "Longer trails with a low radiant (DJ5HG)", off by default, in Efficiency calculation: DJ5HG [18] observes that with a low radiant fewer meteors fall per km² but the trails are longer (up to 100 km usable) and satisfy the reflection law more often. With the option on the weight becomes min(sin El, 15/100): the usable thickness of the layer, 15 km, divided by the sine of the elevation gives the trail length, limited to 100 km. Above about 9° of elevation the two effects cancel out; with the option off, the calculation stays cautious.
TAG|SCELTA|Calculation presets, on the same row: "Cautious" (the default, the most cautious values: height from the radar, normal trails, with β), "As DJ5HG" (102 km, long trails, with β) and "Custom", which is selected automatically when the height, the "from radar" checkbox, the long trails or the angle β are changed by hand.
TAG|RILIEVO|Effect of the angle β on the best direction, 1000 km path: without β the maximum lies between 15° and 90° from the path depending on the radiant elevation (30° with the radiant at 30°, perpendicular with the radiant at 60°) and the perpendicular radiant is worth 67 % with the radiant at 30°; with β the maximum is always with the radiant along the path and the perpendicular radiant drops to 15 %. With β the best-direction rule of SM6FHZ and DH9GCD is therefore lost: this is why β is an option, on by default because it gives the most cautious values (no trail is overestimated).
TAG|FONTE|Two views on the best radiant direction. The classic radio amateur view: the best radiant is between 15° and 70° high and broadside to the path, one aligned with the path is marginal. It is held by SM6FHZ [4] with his experimental rules, by DH9GCD with his formula (reported by DF5HC [16]) and by meteor-mcp from qso-graph [31], which grounds it on the tangency condition: useful trails must be tangent to an ellipsoid with its foci at the two stations, and those running along the path rarely are. This view counts where trails can reflect, but not how much each one reflects.
TAG|FONTE|The full-formula view: McKinley [8] and ITU-R P.843 [24] give the power of each trail the factor 1 / (1 − cos²β sin²φ), where β is the angle between the trail and the path plane. A trail lying in the plane (β = 0) reflects 1/cos²φ times more than one crossing it (β = 90°): 10-15 dB between 600 and 2000 km. With the radiant aligned with the path the tangent trails lie close to the plane and weigh more; with the radiant broadside they cross it and weigh less. DJ5HG [18] uses the same factor, and his hourly curves have the shape that YA-MS finds with β on (shape deviation about 3 dB).
TAG|RILIEVO|The two views use the same specular reflection condition but weight the trails differently: the classic one counts the useful trails, the full one also weighs their power. Over 1000 km, with the radiant at 30°, the perpendicular radiant is worth 67 % without β and 15 % with β. A first comparison with real counts does not tell them apart (next paragraph): deciding needs longer paths and more receivers.
TAG|RILIEVO|Comparison with real counts: BRAMS detections received at Sonneberg (Dourbes beacon, 49.97 MHz, 471 km path) from 12 to 28 August 2026, about 97 000 echoes detected with the method of M. Bundscherer [34]. After removing the background (hour-by-hour median from 22 to 27 August), the Perseids add at most, on 13 August between 02 and 10 UTC, about 80-130 echoes/hour on top of 300-470: at 50 MHz they weigh little compared with the sporadics. The hourly excess follows the YA-MS calculation (Perseid efficiency × ZHR) with a correlation of 0.44 with β on and 0.43 with β off, the same as ZHR times the sine of the radiant elevation; with long trails it drops to 0.36. On this short path the geometry adds no information and the comparison does not decide between the two views. Limits: a single receiver, an adaptive detection threshold that can reduce the counts in the busiest hours, minor showers still active on the background days, beacon antenna pointing at the zenith.
TAG|SCELTA|In the program the box "Angle β between trail and path (full McKinley)" is on by default because it overestimates no trail (cautious value); off, it applies the classic view. The box "Classic verdict" adds under each shower, in the hourly table, the row of the classic verdict computed at the path midpoint: ✗ radiant below the horizon, ◐ marginal (below 15° or above 70°, or less than 30° from the path direction), ● good, ★ best (between 15° and 70° and more than 60° from the path direction), with the thresholds of meteor-mcp [31]. It does not change the calculation: it shows hour by hour where the two views agree and where they do not.
H2|6.3 The percentage
TAG|SCELTA|For each radiant direction the weights of all points where the trail reflects are summed. 100% is the most favourable radiant position for that path:
TAG|SCELTA|Next to the percentage, which applies to that path only, the program gives the efficiency in dB relative to the fixed reference of DJ5HG [18]: 0 dB is the maximum of a 1000 km north-south path straddling the equator with the radiant on the celestial equator at the equinox (the radiant rises in the east, passes through the zenith and sets in the west), calculated with the same height and the same weights. In this way different paths can be compared with each other. DJ5HG states an accuracy better than 6 dB for his values.
EQ|η = 100 · F(radiant) / F(best position)  %
TAG|RILIEVO|Comparison with DJ5HG's QTFGUI (example from its manual): path Hamburg JO53IM - Barcelona, Quadrantids, 4 January. At 07:00 UTC QTFGUI gives pointing 252°, elevation 1°, relative effectiveness -8 dB, radiant in the reflection zone az. 83° el. 75°; at 08:00 no reflection zone; at 09:00 pointing 168°, elevation 2°, -4 dB, radiant az. 292° el. 74°. The program's model (height 102 km like QTFGUI) gives at 07:00 241°, 0°, -5.4 dB, radiant az. 77° el. 74°; at 08:00 0.6 % (-22 dB), i.e. practically zero; at 09:00 161°, 4°, -4.0 dB, radiant az. 286° el. 75°. At the same times DH9GCD gives 26 %, 2 % and 32 %: all three methods see the 08:00 gap, with the radiant almost at the zenith. [17]
TAG|RILIEVO|Comparison with the curves of DJ5HG [18] (Bild 8 and 9: from Hamburg and from Munich, Quadrantids, 4 January), read from the plot (about ±0.5 dB and ±15 min) and compared with the program's automatic test (--prova-dj5hg, one value per hour). Times: the gap with the radiant near the zenith falls in the same hours (Barcelona 7.5–8.5 UTC in DJ5HG, 7–9 in the program; Sofia 6.6–7.8 and 7–8; Narvik 7–8.5 and 7–9); Moscow is closed from 3 to 10 in both, and the program confirms DJ5HG's text: from Hamburg open until about 2:30, from Munich until about 0:30. Pointing: deviations of 2–10° (Barcelona 225° and 233° versus 223° and 235° before the gap, 160° versus 169° after; Moscow 84° versus 82°, 79° versus 76°; Narvik jumps towards 338° at 20:40 in DJ5HG, between 20 and 21 in the program). Levels and shape, at 102 km (mean deviation and typical deviation from the curves): without β and without long trails −1.6 and 4.0 dB; with long trails +1.1 and 4.6 dB; with the angle β −5.4 and 6.2 dB, but with the best shape (3.0 dB once the mean deviation is removed, versus 3.6): the deep minima with a low radiant appear (Barcelona −15.6 dB at 17, versus −16 dB at 19:36; Sofia −18.5 dB at 20, versus −14 dB). With β and long trails ("As DJ5HG" preset) −2.2 and 4.7 dB; the cautious preset (radar height, 89.5 km, with β, without long trails) gives −6.9 and 7.6 dB, with shape 3.3 dB. Without β the program barely sees the minimum with the low radiant almost aligned with the path (−8.6 and −10.4 dB): this is the case of DJ5HG's reservation, which is why the warning remains.
TAG|RILIEVO|The MS-Soft 5.2 documentation [25] defines OH5IY's efficiency as a relative index for underdense trails only, with two factors: radiant elevation (optimum about 45°) and angle with the path (90° = maximum). The minimum is raised by hand to 20 % for overdense trails; the formula is not published. MCG_MS [3] calculates the efficiency for four fixed directions and, when the DX station's locator is entered, also for the real path.
H1|7. How to read the results
H2|7.1 Side of the reflections (A / B)
TAG|SCELTA|For each hour the program calculates which part of the reflections falls to the left of the path (side A) and which to the right (side B). The hourly table shows the letter of the prevailing side.
TAG|FONTE|It is worth shifting the pointing by 10-30° towards that side (side-scatter) [1].
H2|7.2 Path index
TAG|SCELTA|The index sums up in one number how much the showers as a whole favour the path in a given hour:
EQ|I = Σ  ZHR · η / 100   (over all active showers)
H2|7.3 Radiant colours
P|On the Sky view the radiant dot is yellow with efficiency ≥ 50% and ZHR ≥ 2, orange with efficiency ≥ 20%, dark red below 20%, grey when the shower is almost inactive.
H2|7.4 PREV zones in plan view
TAG|SCELTA|For the time shown the program sums, point by point of the E layer, the contribution of each active shower to the efficiency (the same specular reflection condition as in chapter 5), weighted by the ZHR. The points with at least 3 % of the maximum form the PREV zones, one per side of the path, drawn in plan view as ellipses: centre = weighted mean of the points, axes = twice their spread. This is the practical rule: if the shower moves from north to south, the useful trails fall to the east and west. The yellow line shows the direction of motion (radiant azimuth) of the selected shower, or of the most favourable one.
H2|7.5 Section seen from the antenna
TAG|SCELTA|The section shows the same trails seen from the station with the antenna pointed towards the DX station: horizontally the azimuth relative to the DX station (A on the left, B on the right), vertically the elevation. For each shower the 14 strongest reflection points are taken; each trail is a segment oriented towards the radiant, from 10 km above to 10 km below the trail height (at most 60 km per side), thicker the more probable it is. Also drawn are the antenna lobe (−3 dB circle around the lobe elevation), the DX station and Hot A / Hot B.
H1|8. Operating frequency and path loss
H2|8.1 Rules between bands
TAG|FONTE|Experimental rules of SM6FHZ [4]: the number and intensity of reflections scale with λ³, the duration with λ². In forward scatter the duration also increases with sec²φ (τ = λ²·sec²φ / 16π²D, McKinley [8]; OH5IY [25]): the Collm radar measures in backscatter (φ = 0), so on the path the duration must be multiplied by sec²φ at the midpoint (×4 at 60°, ×8.5 at 70°, ×33 at 80°).
TAG|RILIEVO|Real measurements (HamSCI, Meteor Scatter QSO Party [29]): R. Suggs NN4NT measured the power decay constant of 1298 underdense echoes in the MSK144 recordings of NN4SA (EM64) during the 2026 Perseids (4736 WSJT-X WAV files). Medians: 148 ms at 28.145 MHz (41 echoes), 78 ms at 50.260 MHz (1198), 53 ms at 144.150 MHz (59). With λ² starting from 6 m they would be about 245 ms and 9.5 ms: on 2 m pings last more than five times longer than predicted. The author attributes this to a probable echo ceiling height effect on 2 m: at high frequencies mainly the low trails remain visible, where diffusion is slower. It is a preliminary analysis: a single receiving station, different distances not normalised with sec²φ, only echoes strong enough to be detected.
TAG|FONTE|Weitzen and Ralston [27]: the decay constant varies from trail to trail with an exponential distribution, with mean τ = λ²·sec²φ / (32π²D) (power constant; 16π² applies to the amplitude). Xia, Li and Zheng [28] give D as a function of height, D = 10^(0.067·h − 5.6) m²/s: over 1000 km with the trail at 95 km this gives τ ≈ 47 ms at 144 MHz and 390 ms at 50 MHz. The 144 MHz value is close to the measured median, the 50 MHz one is five times higher: D grows tenfold every 15 km of height, so the height of the visible trails, which changes with frequency, weighs on the duration more than λ².
TAG|SCELTA|The program offers the choice "Ping duration between bands" in the Geometry and path loss sub-tab. Theoretical (default, cautious): λ², which gives the shortest pings. Observed: linear interpolation on a logarithmic scale between the three NN4SA medians; below 28 MHz the 10–6 m segment is extended, above 144 MHz, where there are no measurements, λ² is used again. With the observed choice a 1 s echo at Collm (36.2 MHz) lasts about 473 ms at 144 MHz instead of 63 ms, and from 50 to 144 MHz pings become 1.5 times shorter instead of 8.
TAG|RILIEVO|In McKinley [8] λ³ is the power of a single underdense trail, not the number of trails. At a fixed threshold and with mass index s, the number of trails above the threshold scales with λ raised to 1.5·(s − 1), i.e. about λ^1.5 for s = 2 (IZ5TEK [26]; OH5IY [25]). The program keeps k = 3, the experimental rule of SM6FHZ [4], and says so: with k = 3, 1000 meteors/hour at Collm become about 16 reflections/hour at 144 MHz, with k = 1.5 about 127. RMOB observers of the GRAVES radar at 143.050 MHz [13] typically count 20 to 140 echoes/hour; the right value of k for one's own station is obtained from the comparison with RMOB (next section).
TAG|FONTE|Maximum ping duration according to IARU Region 1: 1000 ms at 50 MHz, 500 ms at 70 MHz, 100 ms at 144 MHz, 13 ms at 432 MHz [1].
H2|8.2 From the radar to the amateur band
EQ|N(f) = N(36,2 MHz) · (36,2 / f)^k · K        with k = 3
TAG|RILIEVO|The value of k for one's own station is obtained from the comparison with an RMOB observer on the same band: k = 3 is the SM6FHZ rule, the theory for underdense trails with mass index 2 gives about 1.5.
TAG|SCELTA|The "k from population indices" button computes for each active shower k = 1.5·(s − 1) with s = 1 + 2.5·log10 r, i.e. k = 3.75·log10 r, and takes the ZHR-weighted mean. The population index r comes from the table on the Global Meteor Network flux page [33] when available, otherwise from the IMO catalogue [5]; the Showers tab shows k for each shower. Examples: Perseids r = 2.2, k = 1.28; Geminids r = 2.6, k = 1.56. The default stays k = 3 from SM6FHZ [4], more cautious; the Collm counts, however, are mostly sporadic meteors, for which GMN does not publish r.
TAG|FONTE|Global Meteor Network counts [33]: in the Live showers tab and in the "GMN today" column of the Showers tab the program reads the daily summary of trajectories confirmed by the network's cameras (one line per meteor, with the IAU shower code or "..." for sporadics) and counts them by shower. It is a count, not a flux: it depends on how many stations have a clear, dark sky. Data CC BY 4.0.
H2|8.3 Path loss
EQ|L = 10 · log10 [ 16π² · R1 · R2 · (R1 + R2) · (1 − cos²β · sin²φ) / (λ³ · q² · re²) ] − 10 · log10 a1        a1 = exp[ −8π² · r0² / (λ² · sec²φ) ]
TAG|FONTE|McKinley [8] and Recommendation ITU-R P.843 [24]: β angle between the trail and the plane of the path, a1 initial-radius factor of the trail, with log10 r0 = 0,035 h − 3,45 (r0 in m, h in km). The budget uses β = 90°, the least favourable case (a cautious value), and also shows the best case, β = 0.
TAG|FONTE|References: 172–190 dB from G4BAO's calculations, quoted by GM8JBJ (from Kaiser & Closs [9]); about 200 dB on average at 144 MHz (OH5IY); minimum receivable signal −130 dBm [1].
TAG|RILIEVO|With q = 10¹⁴ electrons/m, trails at 89.5 km and β = 0, at 144 MHz the result is 182–192 dB between 600 and 2100 km (185 dB at 1000 km). The range of 172–190 dB calculated by John Worsnop G4BAO in his doctoral thesis, quoted by GM8JBJ [9], includes these values. With β = 90° the loss increases by 10 dB at 600 km, 13 dB at 1000 km and 16 dB at 2100 km: OH5IY's average of 200 dB cited in the article [1] lies between the two cases. Again according to GM8JBJ, G4BAO finds about 6 dB less for trails aligned with the path.
TAG|RILIEVO|The 182 dB of GM8JBJ's table [9] are not a confirmation: the table does not state the frequency, its 86.4 dB per leg are the free-space loss over 500 km at 1 MHz (the 20 log f term is missing: at 144 MHz it is 129.6 dB) and the sum of the entries, 86.4 + 0 − 9 + 86.4, is 163.8 dB and not 182. The page mentions neither a minimum value nor 144 MHz.
TAG|RILIEVO|Initial radius at the mean echo height, h = 124 − 17 log f [24]: r0 = 0.76 m at 95 km for 50 MHz, 0.40 m at 87 km for 144 MHz, 0.21 m at 79 km for 432 MHz. Over 1000 km a1 is 0.2 dB at 50 MHz, 0.6 dB at 144 MHz and 1.4 dB at 432 MHz (2.9 dB at 600 km). At DJ5HG's fixed height of 102 km it would give about 70 dB at 432 MHz, i.e. no echo: this is why the mean echo height at the frequency is used.
TAG|RILIEVO|Uncertainty of the initial radius: Stober et al. [30], with the CMOR radar at 17.45, 29.85 and 38.15 MHz and a full wave scattering model, find between 85 and 95 km r0 of 0.5–2 m, mostly 1–1.5 m, with a scatter of a factor of two at each height; for transverse polarisation the values are about twice the earlier measurements (Greenhow and Hall 1960; Jones and Campbell-Brown 2005). Only about 5 % of echoes can be described by the ideal model. The ITU-R P.843 formula [24] gives 0.5 m at 90 km and 0.75 m at 95 km, i.e. the lower limit of these measurements.
TAG|SCELTA|The program offers the choice "Initial trail radius" in the Geometry and path loss sub-tab: twice the ITU value (default, cautious, like the measurements of Stober et al.), ITU-R P.843, excluded. Doubling r0 multiplies the factor a1 in dB by 4: over 1000 km 0.8 dB at 50 MHz, 2.4 dB at 144 MHz and 5.6 dB at 432 MHz (11.6 dB at 600 km). The CMOR measurements are at lower frequencies and at 85–95 km: using them at 432 MHz, where echoes come from about 79 km, is an extrapolation.
H1|9. Known limitations
LI|The model considers only underdense trails. Overdense trails (the long bursts) and the sporadic background do not enter the efficiency; the sporadic background is represented by the radar flux.
LI|In the efficiency calculation the antennas are considered isotropic; the real lobe is used only for the Hot check.
LI|The radiant drifts and the B coefficients of the activity profile are working values.
LI|The percentages should be compared with MCG_MS, MS-Soft and with on-air observations.
